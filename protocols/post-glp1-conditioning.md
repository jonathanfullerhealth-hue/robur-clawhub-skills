# Post-GLP-1 Conditioning Protocol

**A structured exercise and monitoring protocol for preserving lean mass, bone density, and
physical function in adults receiving GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy.**

Version 1.0 — September 2026
Author: Jonathan Fuller, BScKin, MScPT

---

## 0. Scope and intended use

**Intended user:** a qualified exercise professional — physiotherapist, clinical exercise
physiologist, kinesiologist — working alongside a prescribing clinician. This is a
professional protocol, not a consumer program.

**This protocol covers:** exercise prescription, physical function assessment, load
progression, monitoring, and escalation criteria.

**This protocol explicitly does not cover, and the exercise professional does not perform:**

- Prescribing, titrating, holding, or discontinuing GLP-1 therapy
- Medical nutrition therapy or individualized diet prescription — protein targets here are
  *reinforced*, not *set*, by the exercise professional; targets come from the prescriber or
  a registered dietitian
- Diagnosis or management of GLP-1 adverse events
- Interpretation of DEXA or laboratory results for clinical decision-making

**The division of labour is the point.** These patients are under-supported precisely
because the prescriber has no exercise pathway and the exercise professional has no line to
the prescriber. This protocol is the bridge, and it only works if each party stays in lane.

---

## 1. The clinical problem

GLP-1 receptor agonists produce weight loss of a magnitude previously seen only after
bariatric surgery — without the standardized multidisciplinary follow-up that bariatric
programs built over decades.

The consequences are measurable:

- An estimated **30–40% of total weight lost on GLP-1 therapy is fat-free mass**, with lean
  mass declines of 10–15% in patients losing more than 15% of body weight.
- Hip bone mineral density falls roughly **2–3%** in the most direct trials, tracking the
  magnitude of weight lost rather than any direct skeletal toxicity. Monotherapy appears
  broadly bone-neutral; dual agonists may carry higher risk, plausibly through greater
  weight loss and larger lean mass reduction.
- **41% of current GLP-1 users have never performed strength training**, and 17% have
  *decreased* it since starting. Only **20%** report referral to a dietitian.

Lower protein intake, older age, and female sex are each associated with greater muscle loss
in this population. Those three factors define the high-risk phenotype.

**State the problem accurately, because prescribers know the counter-evidence.** The DEXA
substudy of STEP 1 found that the *proportion* of lean body mass increased on semaglutide,
because fat mass fell further than lean mass did. That is not in conflict with the figures
above: absolute fat-free mass falls substantially while relative body composition improves.
The clinical concern here is absolute functional capacity in patients with limited reserve —
older adults, women, and those with low baseline muscle — not the body composition ratio.
Framing GLP-1 therapy as uniformly damaging to body composition is unsupported and will cost
credibility in the first conversation with a medical director.

**The intervention is not novel or exotic.** Resistance training plus adequate protein
preserves lean mass during energy restriction — this is among the better-established findings
in exercise science. In one Danish trial, **exercise alone fully preserved hip and spine bone
density during weight loss.** The gap is not knowledge. It is delivery.

## 2. Risk stratification

Stratify at intake. This determines assessment frequency and minimum training dose.

| Tier | Criteria (any one) | Assessment interval | Minimum resistance dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High** | Age ≥65; postmenopausal with known low BMD; baseline sarcopenia or probable sarcopenia; prior fragility fracture; on a dual agonist; targeting >15% total body weight loss; resistance-training naive with BMI ≥40 | 4 weeks | 3×/week, supervised where possible |
| **Moderate** | Age 50–64; female and premenopausal with rapid loss; resistance-training naive; protein intake historically <1.0 g/kg; concurrent insulin or sulfonylurea | 6 weeks | 2–3×/week |
| **Standard** | Age <50, training-experienced, no risk features above | 12 weeks | 2×/week |

Re-stratify if the patient's status changes — a dose escalation to a dual agonist, a new
fracture, or a sustained rapid-loss phase all move a patient up a tier.

## 3. Screening and referral criteria

### Before the first session

Confirm with the prescriber, in writing:

1. Current agent, dose, and titration schedule
2. Concurrent medications, **specifically insulin or sulfonylureas** — these carry
   exercise-associated hypoglycaemia risk and require a glucose plan before training
3. Cardiovascular clearance appropriate to intended intensity
4. Baseline DEXA if available, and whether one is planned
5. Who owns the nutrition plan, and their protein target for this patient
6. Any contraindication to loaded or impact exercise

### Stop the session and refer same-day

- Chest pain, syncope or near-syncope, or new significant dyspnoea
- Severe abdominal pain, particularly radiating to the back (query pancreatitis)
- Right upper quadrant pain, particularly after fatty meals (query gallbladder)
- Persistent vomiting, or any sign of significant dehydration
- Symptomatic hypoglycaemia
- New fall with injury, or suspected fracture

### Escalate to the prescriber within one week

- Grip strength decline >10% from baseline
- New inability to rise from a chair without using the arms
- Protein intake below 0.8 g/kg for two consecutive weeks
- Sustained loss exceeding ~1% of body weight per week beyond the initial weeks
- Any new fall without injury

These are flags for a clinical conversation, not exercise-side decisions. Report them; do not
act on them alone.

## 4. Assessment battery

Chosen for validated cut-points, low equipment cost, and repeatability in a clinic room.

### Core battery — every assessment

| Measure | Method | Threshold of concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Handgrip strength** | Calibrated dynamometer, best of 3, dominant hand, seated, elbow 90° | <27 kg men, <16 kg women (EWGSOP2); or >10% decline from personal baseline |
| **5× sit-to-stand** | Arms crossed, standard chair, timed | >15 seconds; or inability without arms |
| **Gait speed** | 4 m course, usual pace, flying start | ≤0.8 m/s |
| **Body mass** | Same scale, same conditions | Rate of loss, not absolute value |
| **Protein intake** | 3-day recall or app export, reviewed with the nutrition owner | <1.2 g/kg/day |
| **Training adherence** | Sessions completed ÷ prescribed | <80% |

### Extended — baseline, then 6-monthly in high-tier patients

- **Body composition.** DEXA is the reference standard. Appendicular lean mass index below
  7.0 kg/m² (men) or 5.5 kg/m² (women) meets the EWGSOP2 low-muscle-mass criterion.
- **BIA caveat, and it matters here.** Bioimpedance is sensitive to hydration status, and
  GLP-1 patients are frequently under-hydrated from reduced intake and GI losses. BIA in this
  population will over-read fat-free mass loss when the patient is dry. Use it for trend only,
  under standardized conditions, and never as the basis for a clinical decision.
- **DEXA for bone** in high-tier patients, per the prescriber.

### The KPI that matters

**Track strength, not scale weight, and not body composition alone.**

Energy deficiency impairs resistance-training gains in *lean mass* but not in *strength* —
patients can and should get measurably stronger while losing weight. That makes strength the
most responsive, most motivating, and most clinically meaningful signal available. A patient
whose grip and sit-to-stand improve while their weight falls is succeeding, regardless of
what the scale says on a given morning.

## 5. The training protocol

### Governing principles

1. **Volume goes up as the dose goes up.** The instinct is to back off as the patient eats
   less and feels worse. Do the opposite. The therapeutic-dose window is the highest-risk
   period for lean mass, and it is precisely when training load must be protected. Evidence in
   energy restriction favours maintaining or increasing resistance volume over reducing it —
   though note that the specific benefit of *high* volume is not settled (see references C2
   vs C3). What is well supported is that some resistance training is essential and that
   reducing it during the deficit is the error. Hence a firm 2×/week floor, escalating to
   3×/week by risk tier, rather than maximal volume for everyone.
2. **Two sessions a week is the floor, not the target.** Even low-volume resistance training
   prevents lean mass loss during energy restriction. Two well-executed full-body sessions
   beat an aspirational five-day split that gets abandoned in week three.
3. **Anchor the schedule to the titration calendar.** GI symptoms cluster in the 5–10 days
   after each dose escalation. Plan technique acquisition and load progression for the stable
   weeks; plan maintenance for escalation weeks.
4. **Autoregulate by RPE, not by percentage of 1RM.** Body mass, appetite, and hydration are
   all moving. Percentage-based loading against a stale 1RM is meaningless here.

### Phase structure

**Phase 0 — Baseline (before first dose, or week 1 if already started)**
Full assessment battery. Movement screen. Teach the six patterns: squat, hinge, horizontal
push, horizontal pull, vertical pull or press, loaded carry. No meaningful load. Establish
the protein conversation with whoever owns nutrition. Set expectations explicitly: *the goal
is to get stronger while you lose weight.*

**Phase 1 — Acquisition (weeks 1–4, lowest dose)**
Appetite is usually still adequate and symptoms mild. Use this window.
- 2×/week full body, all six patterns
- 2 sets × 8–12 reps, RPE 6–7 (4 reps in reserve)
- Priority is technique and habit, not load
- Add 20–30 minutes of easy aerobic work on 2 other days

**Phase 2 — Escalation (weeks 5–12)**
Symptoms fluctuate with each dose step.
- 2–3×/week full body
- 3 sets × 6–12 reps, RPE 7–8 (2–3 reps in reserve)
- Progressive overload in stable weeks; hold load in the week following an escalation
- Introduce loaded carries and, where safe, low-volume impact loading for bone (heel drops,
  step-downs, low-height hops) — **contraindicated in known low BMD or prior fragility
  fracture without prescriber clearance**

**Phase 3 — Therapeutic dose / peak loss (weeks 13–24)**
The highest-risk window. Lean mass loss is steepest here.
- **3×/week resistance, non-negotiable in high and moderate tiers**
- 3–4 sets × 6–10 reps on primary lifts, RPE 8 (2 reps in reserve)
- Increase total weekly sets per muscle group relative to Phase 2
- Assessment interval tightens per risk tier
- If adherence drops below 80%, treat it as a clinical event and escalate — do not simply
  reduce the program

**Phase 4 — Maintenance / plateau (week 25+)**
Weight loss slows; the patient feels better.
- 3×/week, undulating intensity across the week
- Reintroduce genuine progressive overload
- Shift emphasis toward capacity and function — carries, stairs, sled work, sport-specific
  or occupational tasks
- Full battery every 12 weeks

**Phase 5 — Discontinuation (the neglected phase)**
Appetite returns, often abruptly. Weight regain in this window is disproportionately fat
unless training is already established.
- **Maintain resistance training at full Phase 4 volume through discontinuation and for at
  least 12 weeks afterward.** Do not taper training as the drug tapers.
- Re-assess at 4, 12, and 24 weeks post-discontinuation
- This phase is where the patient either keeps the body composition they worked for or
  loses it. Say so plainly at the outset.

## 6. Nutrition guardrails

**Your role is reinforcement and reporting, not prescription.** Targets come from the
prescriber or dietitian. What follows is what you monitor and echo.

- **Protein: 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**, evenly distributed, **20–30 g per feeding**
- **Distribution is the whole practical problem.** Delayed gastric emptying makes large
  protein boluses uncomfortable or impossible. Five feedings of 25 g is achievable where
  three of 40 g is not. This single reframe resolves most intake failures.
- **Energy deficit ≤500 kcal/day** where the prescriber is targeting a controlled rate.
  Larger deficits worsen lean mass loss.
- **Calcium 1,000–1,200 mg/day and vitamin D** per the prescriber, for bone protection
  alongside loading.
- **Hydration.** Reduced intake plus GI losses makes dehydration common. It affects both
  training tolerance and the reliability of any BIA measure.

If protein is below target for two consecutive weeks, that is a referral, not a coaching
problem. Suboptimal protein intake is documented and common in this population, and it is the
single strongest modifiable predictor of muscle loss you will encounter.

## 7. Monitoring and decision rules

Deterministic triage, applied at every assessment. Rules first; clinical judgement second.

```
RED — escalate to prescriber within one week; do not progress load
  • Grip strength decline > 10% from baseline
  • New inability to rise from chair without arms
  • 5× sit-to-stand > 15 seconds where previously below
  • Gait speed ≤ 0.8 m/s
  • Protein < 0.8 g/kg for 2 consecutive weeks
  • Sustained loss > ~1% body weight/week beyond initial weeks
  • Training adherence < 60% (program disengagement — a clinical event, per §5)
  • Any fall

YELLOW — hold load, address the driver, reassess in 2 weeks
  • Grip strength decline 5–10% from baseline
  • Protein 0.8–1.2 g/kg
  • Adherence 60–79%
  • GI symptoms limiting intake or training
  • Two or more consecutive sessions missed

GREEN — progress per phase
  • Strength maintained or improving
  • Protein ≥ 1.2 g/kg
  • Adherence ≥ 80%
  • No new functional decline
```

A RED flag is a communication trigger. It does not authorize the exercise professional to
alter medication, diet prescription, or medical management.

## 8. Documentation and clinic reporting

The reporting layer is what makes this saleable to a practice, and it is what earns the
renewal.

**Per patient, per assessment:** the core battery, tier, phase, adherence, triage status, and
any flags raised with dates and recipients.

**Per clinic, per quarter — aggregate and de-identified:**

- Mean change in grip strength and 5× sit-to-stand across the cohort
- Proportion maintaining or improving strength during active weight loss
- Proportion meeting protein target
- Adherence distribution
- Flags raised and their resolution
- Functional decline events

That quarterly report is the artifact that demonstrates the program is working. It is also
the dataset that, with appropriate ethics approval and consent, supports genuine prospective
research in a population where almost none exists.

## 9. Contraindications and cautions

**Absolute — do not train until cleared:**
Suspected pancreatitis or gallbladder disease; unresolved chest pain or syncope; acute
severe dehydration or persistent vomiting; suspected fracture.

**Relative — modify and coordinate with the prescriber:**
Known low BMD or prior fragility fracture (avoid impact loading and spinal flexion under
load); concurrent insulin or sulfonylurea (glucose plan required before training);
symptomatic orthostatic hypotension; active GI symptoms limiting intake; retinopathy or
other diabetes complications affecting exercise selection.

**Full annotated bibliography with evidence grading: [`references.md`](references.md).**

The load-bearing sources:

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| All assessment cut-points | Cruz-Jentoft et al., *Age and Ageing* 2019;48(1):16–31 (EWGSOP2) — A1 |
| 30–40% of loss is fat-free mass | *Metabolites* 2026;16(6):364 — B1 |
| Proportional lean mass improves (the counterpoint) | Wilding et al., STEP 1 DEXA substudy, *J Endocr Soc* 2021 — B2 |
| Strength preserved in deficit; lean mass gains blunted | Meta-analysis, PubMed 34623696 — C1 |
| Maintain or increase resistance volume | PMC9012799 — C2, read against Roth et al. 2023 — C3 |
| Protein 1.2–1.6 g/kg, 20–30 g per feeding | PMC12693348 — D2 |
| ~20% dietitian referral rate | PMC12913018 — D3 |
| Bone: monotherapy neutral, dual agonists higher risk | *Diagnostics* 2026;16(13):2128 — E1 |
| 41% never strength trained | PwC 2026 — F2 |
| 11% of US adults currently using | Gallup 2026 — F1 |

Grading runs Established → Supported → Emerging → Contextual. Nothing here rests on a single
contextual source, and where a threshold derives from emerging evidence the protocol monitors
more closely rather than intervening more aggressively.

## 11. Review

Reassess this protocol against the literature every six months. This is a fast-moving area:
dual and triple agonists, muscle-preserving co-therapies such as bimagrumab, and the first
large functional-outcome trials will all change the picture. Version and date every revision;
clinics need to know which version their staff were trained on.
